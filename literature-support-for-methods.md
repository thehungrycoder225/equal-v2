# Literature support for the preprocessing and embedding methods (2022–2025)

This document links each method used so far in the EQUAL job-ad pipeline to published literature, and states what each source does and does not support. Every reference below was looked up in Crossref (DOI, authors, venue, pages), and abstracts were read for the sources on which the Context and Correlation entries rely. Full texts were not read except where noted, so claims are limited to what the abstracts and metadata state. Please check key claims against the full paper before citing them in a manuscript.

**Scope.** Sources are 2022–2025, as requested. Three older foundational works are listed separately in Section 3 because the pipeline directly uses their methods. Updated after Stage B (M9, run), and Step 6 (M10–M12: explicit and implicit detection and job-ad labelling), with new references [23] and [24].

## 2. Method discussion with literature support

### 2.1 Corpus audit and duplicate handling (M1)

Job-ad corpora contain repeated posts and shared templates. We flagged exact and normalized duplicates and kept every record, because a record can be a legitimate re-post with different surrounding context. Duplicates and near-duplicates are a known source of distortion in language-model data: Lee et al. [9] found many near-duplicate examples and repeated substrings in common datasets, and that over 4% of validation sets overlapped with training data. In our data, 3,264 of 8,005 sentence units are exact repeats of another unit, so repeated template lines will dominate some clusters. Because the same repetition can leak across a train–test split, we plan to split by employer, which is consistent with the leakage taxonomy of Kapoor and Narayanan [14]. Our decision to retain duplicates is a design choice that these sources motivate but do not mandate.

### 2.2 Structural parsing (M2)

We separated each ad into job title, qualification block, contact block, source URL and other body text so that later steps can treat them differently. We found no 2022–2025 source that validates this exact parsing, so it is justified procedurally. Related work argues that job-ad analysis should go beyond single-word matching and look at the content that describes requirements [6], [1]. Step 1 should therefore be presented as a documented, rule-based preparation step, with its error rate checked by manual sampling.

### 2.3 Masking employer, contact and source fields (M3)

We wanted the embeddings and clusters to reflect wording and not the employer. Dikshit et al. [1] describe a standardization step with the same aim; according to the excerpt supplied by the project team, they replace named entities and academic titles with standard tokens. We adapted this: we masked the employer name (and its suffix-free form), the contact block, and the source URL with typed placeholders, kept the originals as restricted metadata, and kept the job title and qualification text because they are the wording under study. The Text Anonymization Benchmark [10] frames masking as an editing task that must be evaluated for disclosure, not assumed to work. Our check found no residual employer or contact string, which shows the masks were applied consistently. It does not show that re-identification is impossible: abbreviations, branch names and repeated employer-specific template lines remain (see the Step 2 limitations).

### 2.4 Text normalization (M4)

We lowercased the retained text, removed line-leading bullet markers, and collapsed whitespace and line breaks, while keeping other punctuation and every word, including gender-marked occupational nouns such as "barista" and "mechanic". Siino et al. [16] report that preprocessing choices can change classification performance even with modern Transformers, so we treat normalization as a documented decision and measured its effect (Section 2.8). We did not remove stop words, stem, or strip punctuation. Hu et al. [4] note that removing gender-explicit words does not fully address gender bias because implicit traits also carry it, and Borchers et al. [2] work with gendered role wording directly; both are consistent with keeping wording intact. Note that the Step 3 text is lowercase and bullet-free, and was therefore **not** used for sentence segmentation or embedding; those steps used the Step 2 text so line boundaries and case were preserved.

### 2.5 Sentence-level segmentation (M5)

The analysis unit is the sentence or bullet, as in Dikshit et al. [1]. We split on line breaks and on `.`, `!`, `?` followed by whitespace, which is transparent but simpler than a trained sentence tokenizer and can split abbreviations or keep fragments. It produced 8,005 units from 1,500 ads, against 15,914 units in the earlier notebook. The two segmentation rules should be reconciled and one chosen, because unit counts affect every downstream number. Units are analysis segments and not independent observations; units from one ad or employer are correlated.

### 2.6 Pretrained multilingual sentence embeddings (M6)

We embedded each unique sentence with `paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2` from the SentenceTransformers framework, without further training, and L2-normalized the vectors so the dot product is the cosine similarity. A multilingual model was chosen because the ads may mix English and Filipino; multilingual sentence-embedding work such as Feng et al. [11] supports the general approach, and Cruz and Cheng [22] indicate Filipino-specific resources exist. We have not tested whether our model handles Filipino or Taglish well. Muennighoff et al. [12] found that no single embedding model is best across tasks, which is why we validated the embeddings on our own text (Section 2.8). We did not compare against other encoders such as Sentence-T5 [20] or LaBSE [11], so the model choice remains a limitation. Dikshit et al. [1] used a different model (`all-mpnet-base-v2`, per the supplied excerpt), so results are not directly comparable.

### 2.7 Exploratory clustering (M7)

We ran K-means (k = 6) on the embeddings and on TF-IDF features, as Dikshit et al. [1] cluster contrastive embeddings and as Petukhova et al. [18] show that embedding and algorithm choice change clustering results. The two partitions agree little (adjusted Rand index 0.145). The embedding clusters read as requirement types: titles, gender lines, soft skills, education, location, and experience. Silhouette scores (0.155 for embeddings, 0.058 for TF-IDF) are low and come from different feature spaces, so they are not a basis for choosing k or for claiming natural groups. The clusters are descriptive and are not agentic/communal labels.

### 2.8 Semantic-preservation validation (M8)

Before fine-tuning, we tested whether the embeddings keep the distinctions a gender-bias study needs. The design has four parts, none of which needs gold labels:

- **Invariance.** Formatting-only edits should not move a sentence. Result: whitespace and trailing punctuation gave cosine about 1.00; bullet markers 0.99; lowercase 0.99; UPPERCASE 0.87 (self-retrieval 73%).
- **Sensitivity.** Meaning-changing edits should move a sentence. Result: gender swap 0.79 (17 corpus pairs only), "years + 4" 0.96, "With" → "Without" 0.91, against 0.21 for random pairs. Hand-built pairs agreed (e.g., "Preferably Male" vs "Preferably Female" 0.78; "At least" vs "At most" 1 year 0.91). Truong et al. [15] document negation insensitivity in large language models, which is consistent with this but is not a test of sentence encoders. Dolci [19] motivates gender-swap pairs, in that case to enforce similarity between them.
- **Probes.** Rule-derived attributes can be recovered from the embeddings with grouped cross-validation, following the caveats on probing classifiers in Belinkov [13] and the leakage guidance in [14]. Macro-F1 on years of experience was 0.85 (embedding) vs 0.72 (TF-IDF); education level 0.94 vs 0.97; gender wording 0.85 vs 0.84, but with only 31 sentences.
- **Human review.** A stratified file of 120 nearest-neighbour pairs is prepared; it has **not** yet been judged.

Interpretation, kept within what was measured: the embeddings preserve topic and tolerate formatting changes, but they do not separate opposite or numerically different requirements well. Consistent with Gallegos et al. [17] and Nemani et al. [21], pretrained encoders may also carry social bias, and embedding similarity is not a bias judgment.

### 2.9 Stage B: contrastive fine-tuning (M9)

We ran unsupervised SimCSE [F2] on the training employers (256 of 320) and tested on the 64 held-out employers. The literature offers three consistent options: counterfactual gender-swapped pairs [19], [24], supervised contrastive fine-tuning on curated or labelled data [2], and contrastive embeddings for job-ad language [1]. Unsupervised SimCSE belongs to none of the bias-specific options, since it has no gender signal. The result matched that expectation. It lowered every cosine value, including those for true paraphrases (0.908 → 0.786), the random-pair median fell from 0.204 to 0.031, and gender-swap pairs barely moved (0.793 → 0.772). The years-of-experience probe improved (macro-F1 0.695 → 0.917) but on only 101 sentences. We did not adopt this model. A choice is still needed: pulling gender-swapped pairs together makes the embedding less sensitive to gender wording, which suits debiasing but not detection. Kumar et al. [3] and Mihaljević et al. [5] show job-ad wording is a legitimate target and that automated tools disagree, which supports keeping human validation in the loop.

### 2.10 Explicit gender detection (M10)

We flag ads that state a gender requirement. A sentence is "restrictive" when it names only men or only women, "inclusive" when it names both (for example "Male/Female"), and we also count gendered occupational titles (for example watchman, foreman, 29 ads), he/she pronouns (3 ads), and bare gendered nouns such as "Farm Boy" or "Steel man" (3 ads), which are retained and reported separately from restrictions. Explicit wording is the case where rules are most dependable, and detection of such labels is framed as a classification task validated against human annotation in the job-description literature [23]. In our 1,500 ads, 207 are male-restricted, 112 female-restricted and 102 inclusive, so explicit restrictions lean towards men about 1.8 to 1. A first version wrongly flagged "MAN" in "LAN, WAN, MAN" (a network type) and counted "Steel man" as a restriction; both were found during counter-validation and corrected. Because tools that look alike produce different outputs on the same ads [5], these counts are exploratory until a sample is hand-checked. The rules are our own and have no gold labels yet, so precision and recall are unknown. We keep a per-sentence evidence file so that each flag can be reviewed.

### 2.11 Implicit detection (M11)

Implicit bias is wording that signals who is expected to apply without naming a gender. We use two indicators. First, the agentic and communal word stems from Gaucher et al. [F3], matched by prefix, as in [1]. Second, gender-linked proxies treated as separate flags, in line with the multi-category framing of Everitt et al. [23], who also separate age and other exclusionary wording from gender wording: marital status or pregnancy (48 ads), age limits (21) and appearance or physical wording (92). Two cautions apply. The word lists were designed for US-English ads, and lexicon work in other languages argues that lists need local adaptation [8]. In our ads the most frequent hits are duty words (analytical, leadership, interpersonal, honest, responsible), which may describe the job and not signal gender. We excluded "confidential*" because it shares the "confident" stem. We have not tested the Gaucher list on Philippine ads.

### 2.12 Job-ad labelling by coded-word balance (M12)

Following Dikshit et al. [1], an ad with more masculine-coded than feminine-coded words is labelled agentic (masculine-coded), one with more feminine-coded words communal (feminine-coded), and one with equal counts balanced. We add a fourth class, "no signal", for ads with no coded word at all. Without it the balanced class would absorb 1,090 ads that carry no evidence either way, since our ads are short (median 32 words). Result: 193 communal, 164 agentic, 53 balanced, 1,090 no signal. The dictionary label does not predict explicit bias: of the 164 agentic ads, 14 are male-restricted. We did not adopt the second technique of [1], k-means on average sentence embeddings, because our clusters group by job family and agree with the dictionary labels at chance (adjusted Rand index −0.04), so cluster names would be arbitrary. [1] was developed on academic ads, which are longer than ours, and I have not verified its results against the full paper.

### 2.13 Annotation schema and sample (M13)

Step 8 defines a three-layer scheme. Each sentence/requirement unit (7,971 units from 1,500 ads) gets a bias type (explicit, implicit, neutral statement, none, unclear), one or more protected attributes, and the phrase carrying the bias. Bias presence is derived from the type. Explicit bias is a stated restriction, including "Female/Male" wording, age limits and civil-status requirements; implicit bias is coded wording or a proxy, such as agentic or communal traits and appearance requirements. The category design follows prior job-ad taxonomies [25], [23] and the explicit/implicit and span structure of the JABD dataset [27], which has no peer-reviewed paper. Unlike JABD, whose guide requires mutually exclusive categories, we allow several attributes per unit because one requirement can combine them (for example "pleasing personality, female and single"). The presence, type and attribute hierarchy parallels [28], and span tagging that includes job-hiring text appears in [26]. Typos and grammar errors go into a separate quality flag and never change the bias label; the nearest evidence is [30], where lexical content mattered more than grammar. Rewrites replace biased wording with neutral wording, in line with [31]. The sample has 596 units from 469 ads and 208 employers, drawn from rule-based strata with at most three units per employer per stratum (inclusive_wording 86 of a target of 102 and proxy_marital 30 of 40 fell short because of that cap). The rule flags select candidates and are not labels. Inter-annotator agreement is deferred to a later phase. No human labels exist yet.

### 2.14 Rewrite recommender: rule-based baseline for explicit bias (M14)

Step 10 proposes a rewrite for each of the 484 units that Step 9 labelled explicit, and leaves every other line unchanged. We rewrote with deterministic rules, not a generative model, because explicit restrictions ("Male", "Single/Married", "25-30 years old") are short and repetitive: 484 units come from only 50 distinct texts. A pure gender line becomes "Open to all qualified candidates", the wording in the project example; a pure marital or age line is deleted; a line with other content keeps that content with the restriction removed; pregnancy, height and weight conditions, and fragments too short to stand alone, go to manual review. Keeping the original text and changing only flagged wording follows the idea of suggesting alternatives closely related to the original in Hu et al. [4]. The check after rewriting is a residual rate: the share of rewritten units in which the detection rules or the Step 9 model still find explicit wording. This is similar in spirit to the bias-word neutrality score of Kumar et al. [37], but it uses our own rules and says nothing about quality, so it is an internal consistency check only. We did not rewrite implicit units. The literature supports two ways to do so, fine-tuning a seq2seq model on pairs [35] or prompting an instruction-following LLM [36], [38], and [36] reports that small fine-tuned models do well with limited data. Neither was trained or run here. We found no 2022–2025 study that rewrites job ads with a generative model and evaluates it with automatic metrics in one place; the literature splits into job-ad detection with guided rewording [4], [37] and general or non-English rewriting models [35], [36], [38]. Human evaluation of readability, neutrality, content preservation and fluency is carried out separately by the research partner and is not reported here.

### 2.15 Rewrite recommender: prompted open-weights LLM with guardrails for appearance wording (M15)

Step 11 rewrites the 91 silver-implicit units (37 distinct texts) that match the appearance rules, with `Qwen/Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct` run on CPU. We used prompting, not training, because we have no human-checked rewrite pairs and prompting-only debiasing is reported to work without retraining [39]; open-weights models are reported to do well on minimal-edit correction tasks under zero- and few-shot prompting [40], and models under 10 billion parameters have been studied for job-description bias detection [41]. The pipeline is fixed, not agentic: one greedy generation per distinct text, with no retries, followed by rule-based checks that reject an output if it still contains appearance wording, adds new coded or protected wording, adds numbers, drops other content, or is too long. A rejected output leaves the original line unchanged and flags it for review, in the same way a detoxification pipeline flags text it cannot safely edit [42]. Height and weight lines go to manual review because they may be genuine job requirements. We reran the first 8 texts to check determinism, since outputs can vary even at fixed settings [43]. The check thresholds are our own design choices, and the checks reuse the same rules that detected the lines, so they test consistency and not quality. Human evaluation is done by the research partner and is not reported here.
